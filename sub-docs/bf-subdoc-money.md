# SECTION ONE: WHERE MONEY COMES FROM

## How Britain Is Currently Funded

Britain raises approximately £1.1 trillion in tax revenue per year (2023-24 figures, approximate).

The main sources:

| Source | Approximate Annual Revenue |
|---|---|
| Income Tax | £270 billion |
| National Insurance | £180 billion |
| VAT | £170 billion |
| Corporation Tax | £90 billion |
| Council Tax | £45 billion |
| Business Rates | £25 billion |
| Fuel Duty | £25 billion |
| Other taxes and duties | ~£295 billion |

**Total: approximately £1.1 trillion**

Government spending is approximately £1.2 trillion per year — the difference is borrowed (the deficit).

Britain Forward does not promise to eliminate the deficit immediately. We promise to make better decisions about what is spent and what is invested, and to add revenue sources that currently flow abroad.

## Britain Forward's Additional Revenue Sources

### Revenue Source 1: Nationalised Asset Returns

Water, energy networks, and oil currently generate profits that flow primarily to:
- Foreign sovereign wealth funds (Norwegian pension fund holds stakes in UK water)
- Private equity firms
- Institutional investors, many registered offshore

**Under Britain Forward:** These returns flow to British Water, British Grid, and the Sovereign Wealth Fund instead.

**Estimated additional revenue:**
- Water sector profits: approximately £2-3 billion per year (currently to private shareholders)
- Energy network profits: approximately £3-5 billion per year
- North Sea oil (British Petroleum plc stake, growing over time): variable, initially smaller
- Total initial additional revenue: approximately £5-8 billion per year, growing as stakes increase

*These are not new taxes. They are existing profits redirected from foreign and private investors to British citizens.*

### Revenue Source 2: Pharmaceutical Price Negotiation

Britain currently pays pharmaceutical companies substantially more than other comparable countries for the same drugs.

**Example:** The NHS pays significantly more per dose for certain cancer treatments than France, Germany, or the Netherlands — despite the drug being identical and partly developed with British public research funding.

A pharmaceutical price negotiation body with genuine bargaining power — modelled on the approach of several EU countries — is estimated by NHS England's own analysis to save £1-2 billion per year initially, rising as the body's bargaining position strengthens.

*This is not rationing. It is paying a fair price instead of an inflated one.*

### Revenue Source 3: Stopping Mismanagement and Corruption

This is harder to quantify precisely, but the categories are real:

**Procurement corruption and cronyism:**
The COVID PPE procurement scandal alone is estimated to have cost £4+ billion in contracts that produced little or no usable equipment. Systematic procurement reform — open competition, published contracts, independent audit — reduces this. Conservative estimate: £500 million - £1 billion per year in reduced waste.

**MP expenses and political self-enrichment:**
The current system allows claims that a reasonable person would not accept as legitimate work costs. Reform saves hundreds of millions per year — not enormous at national scale, but politically important as a signal.

**Benefits administration:**
The DWP spends billions administering means-tested benefits — assessment staff, case managers, appeals tribunals, sanctions reviews. UBI, when introduced, eliminates most of this administrative apparatus. Estimated savings: £5-10 billion per year in administration costs alone (distinct from the benefit payments themselves).

**Total estimated savings from mismanagement reduction: £6-12 billion per year**

*This is not magic. It is stopping spending money on things that do not benefit anyone except the recipients of bad contracts and the administrators of bad systems.*

### Revenue Source 4: Road Charging

As electric vehicles grow, fuel duty — approximately £25 billion per year — declines. A usage-based road charging system replaces this revenue:

- Charged per mile, varying by vehicle type, road type, and time of day
- EVs pay road charge (they currently pay nothing; this is fair and sustainable)
- Revenue hypothecated to road maintenance and public transport
- Net revenue: approximately equivalent to current fuel duty over time

*This is not a new tax. It is replacing one tax (fuel duty) with a fairer one (road usage).*

### Revenue Source 5: UBI Higher-Earner Tax Recoupture

UBI paid to higher earners is recovered through marginal income tax rates. Someone earning £100,000 per year receives £5,200 UBI — but their marginal tax rate means the net transfer is substantially reduced or eliminated.

**Estimated recoupture:** Significant — precise figure requires full economic modelling. The principle is that UBI is largely self-funding at the higher income levels through the existing income tax system.

### Revenue Source 6: Long-Term Economic Growth

Better-educated population → higher productivity → higher tax revenues.
Healthier population → lower NHS crisis costs → lower spending, or same spending with better outcomes.
Better infrastructure → lower business costs → higher corporate profitability → higher corporation tax.
UBI → more entrepreneurship → more businesses → more employment → more income tax.

These are not accounting lines. They are the reason that investment in people and infrastructure has always, over time, produced economic growth that exceeds the cost of the investment.

## What Changes Under Britain Forward

### The Revenue Budget vs The Capital Budget

Britain Forward is explicit about a distinction that most political debate ignores:

**Revenue budget:** Day-to-day spending — wages, benefits, services, running costs. Must be funded from tax revenues. Cannot be borrowed sustainably.

**Capital budget:** Investment in long-lasting assets — roads, rail, buildings, infrastructure. Can be financed through long-term bonds because the asset generates returns or savings over its lifetime. This is how every business and every homeowner finances large purchases.

Conflating these two — treating road investment the same as NHS wage costs — is how "you can't afford it" arguments mislead. A road built once to last thirty years is not the same kind of financial commitment as a salary paid every month.

### Revenue Changes

**What increases:**
- NHS investment: additional £15-20 billion per year target (phased)
- Education: arts programme, SEND reform, curriculum delivery: £3-5 billion additional
- Veterans support: £1-2 billion additional
- Public Dignity Infrastructure (toilets, kitchens, showers, orchards): £500 million - £1 billion
- UBI (net additional cost after recoupture and system savings): independent modelling required — see UBI sub-document

**What decreases:**
- Benefits administration (DWP): £5-10 billion reduction as UBI replaces means-testing
- Procurement waste: £1-2 billion reduction
- NHS crisis costs (preventative care effect, long-term): £3-5 billion reduction over 5-10 years
- Debt interest (as Sovereign Wealth Fund grows and national productivity improves): longer term

### Capital Changes (Bond-Financed Investment)

**The key point:** These are investments, not costs. Each generates returns — financial, economic, or social — that exceed the cost over the investment's lifetime. The roads that currently cost Britain billions in repeated patching are a worse financial deal than roads built once properly, even though the upfront cost is higher.

# SECTION THREE: THE SOVEREIGN WEALTH FUND

## What It Is

A national investment fund that holds assets on behalf of the British people. Returns are used for:
1. Reinvestment in British infrastructure
2. Funding future generations' public services
3. Stabilisation — a buffer against economic shocks

## How It Is Built

**Phase 1 — Seeding (Years 1-5):**
- Initial capitalisation from nationalised asset revenues (water, energy, oil)
- Windfall tax revenues from excess profits in extractive industries
- Target initial fund: £50-100 billion

**Phase 2 — Growth (Years 5-15):**
- Ongoing asset revenues
- Investment returns on existing fund
- British oil surplus sales
- Target: £250-500 billion

**Phase 3 — Maturity (Years 15+):**
- Fund self-sustaining and growing
- Annual returns supplement public services
- Long-term fiscal stability for Britain
- Target: comparable per-capita to Norway over decades

## What It Is Not

- Not a magic pot that funds everything immediately
- Not a substitute for tax revenue in the short term
- Not a promise of Norwegian-scale returns in five years

Norway's fund exists because Norway invested its oil revenues over 40 years rather than spending them. Britain spent its oil revenues. We cannot undo that. We can stop repeating the mistake.

# SECTION FOUR: THE TRANSPARENT SPENDING BREAKDOWN

## What Every British Taxpayer Will See — Annually

A plain-English breakdown of exactly where every pound of tax revenue was spent.

**National level — example format:**

*For every £100 you paid in tax this year:*
- £16 funded the NHS
- £23 funded social protection (benefits and pensions)
- £10 funded education
- £5 funded debt interest payments
- £3 funded defence
- ... and so on

**Local level — example format:**

*For every £100 your council received in council tax and central government grant:*
- £35 funded adult social care
- £20 funded roads and transport
- £18 funded waste collection
- £12 funded children's services
- ... and so on

**Published:** Every library. Every GP surgery. Every job centre. Every post office. Online in accessible, searchable format. In multiple languages.

**Independently verified:** Not government self-reporting. An independent audit body confirms the figures before publication.

**Why this matters:**

The reason people resist high taxation is not selfishness. It's that they don't see the return. In France and Scandinavia, high tax rates have high public acceptance because the benefit is visible, felt, and understood.

In Britain, people pay their tax and have no clear idea where it goes. They feel taxed without feeling served.

Transparency does not guarantee satisfaction. But it makes honest conversation about taxation possible — and it makes it much harder for politicians to waste money quietly.

# SECTION FIVE: HOW WE ANSWER "HOW DO YOU PAY FOR IT?"

## The Full Answer

**Short version (for interviews and debates):**
"We pay for it the same way Norway pays for its public services — by keeping the returns from our national resources in Britain instead of sending them abroad. By stopping wasting money on procurement corruption and administrative complexity. By investing in infrastructure properly once rather than patching it forever. And by the straightforward logic that healthy, educated, housed people cost far less in crisis services than people who are sick, undereducated, and homeless."

**The distinction that matters (always make this):**
"Some of what we propose is spending. Some is investment. A road that lasts thirty years is not the same as a salary — it's a capital asset. We finance long-term assets through long-term bonds, repaid from the returns on those assets. This is how every business, every homeowner, and every responsible government works. Treating infrastructure investment as current spending is how governments make bad decisions."

**When asked for specific numbers:**
"Full independent costings will be commissioned and published before any general election. We are honest that we cannot produce Treasury-grade costings without Treasury-grade data. What we can tell you is the logic: the costs are real, the returns are real, and the net position — over a decade — is better than the alternative of continued patching, continued waste, and continued decline."

**When the gross UBI figure is quoted:**
"The gross figure is not the relevant number. Higher earners have their UBI taxed back. The means-tested benefits system it replaces costs billions to administer. The net additional cost requires independent modelling — which we will commission. Would you like to discuss what the current system costs, or are we only interested in the cost of the alternative?"
