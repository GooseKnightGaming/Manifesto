# THE CORE ARGUMENT

Britain sold its national infrastructure to private investors over several decades. The results are measurable:

- **Water:** Thames Water nearly insolvent; 3 billion litres lost daily through leaking pipes; rivers contaminated by sewage discharge; shareholders paid dividends while infrastructure decayed
- **Energy:** Prices dependent on global markets Britain does not control; profits extracted by foreign-owned companies from British households
- **Rail:** The most expensive fares in Europe; fragmented services; private operators subsidised by the state while keeping profits when times are good
- **Oil:** North Sea reserves extracted by private companies; revenues leaving Britain rather than funding British public services

The privatisation experiment has run for forty years. The evidence is in. It has not delivered the consumer benefits promised. It has delivered profit extraction, infrastructure decay, and national vulnerability.

Britain Forward will reverse this — carefully, legally, and affordably.

# THE THREE-TIER MODEL — DETAILED

## Tier One: Infrastructure Layer — 100% Public Ownership

The *physical networks* — pipes, wires, cables, track — are publicly owned and managed by public bodies.

This covers:
- Water distribution pipes and treatment works → **British Water**
- Electricity transmission and distribution networks → **British Grid**
- Gas transmission networks → **British Gas Networks**
- Rail track, stations, and signalling → **British Rail Infrastructure** (already largely public via Network Rail — this formalises and completes it)
- Telecommunications backbone → **British Digital Infrastructure**

**Why 100% at this tier:**
Natural monopoly. You cannot have competition in pipes and wires — there is one set of pipes under your street, not several competing sets. Without public ownership, a private monopoly extracts rent from a captive population. The evidence — water bills, energy bills, rail fares — proves this happens.

**Direct accountability:**
When water is contaminated, British Water is accountable. One body. The CEO of British Water appears before Parliament. No chain of private company → internal review → independent review → regulator → tribunal → Ofwat enforcement notice. Direct. Clear. Real consequences.

## Tier Two: Operating Layer — Majority Public, Citizen Shares

Companies that *operate services through* the public infrastructure.

- **British Water Services** — delivers water to homes and businesses through British Water's network
- **British Power** — generates and retails electricity using British Grid's network
- **British Rail Operations** — runs passenger services on British Rail Infrastructure's track
- Regional variants where appropriate — Scottish Water already publicly owned; Welsh Water already mutual; these become templates

**Ownership structure:**
- British Government: 51% minimum controlling stake
- British citizens: up to 49% — shares offered to UK residents first, prioritising smaller individual holdings over institutional investors
- Foreign institutional investment: excluded from this tier
- Citizens receive annual dividends proportionate to their shareholding

**Why not 100% public at this tier:**
Some market discipline is maintained. Operating companies can be more or less efficient; the 49% citizen shareholder element creates accountability without profit extraction by foreign investors. Citizens who own shares have an interest in the company performing well — this creates a constituency for good management rather than political management.

## Tier Three: Competitive Layer — Fully Private

Services and products built *on top of* the public infrastructure, competing freely.

- Broadband providers using British Digital Infrastructure's backbone
- Energy service companies (smart meters, efficiency products, solar installation)
- Rail catering, retail, and ancillary services
- Water technology companies (purification, efficiency, smart metering)
- Renewable energy generators selling into British Grid

**Why fully private here:**
This is where innovation happens. Competition at the service and technology level drives improvement, efficiency, and consumer choice. The infrastructure is public. What runs on it can compete.

*The analogy:* The internet runs on publicly-funded backbone infrastructure. Private companies (Google, Amazon, every website) build on top of it and compete freely. Nobody argues the internet should be fully private or that the backbone should be sold. Britain's physical infrastructure follows the same logic.

# HOW WE ACQUIRE IT — THE LEGAL AND FINANCIAL MECHANISM

## The Acquisition Challenge

Critics correctly note that buying major infrastructure companies costs money. Thames Water's regulatory asset base (the independently assessed value of its physical infrastructure) is approximately £16-18 billion. The entire water sector in England: approximately £70 billion RAB value. Energy networks: comparable scale.

This sounds enormous. Here is why it is affordable.

## Mechanism One: Regulatory Asset Base Acquisition

We acquire at **Regulatory Asset Base value** — the independently assessed value of the physical infrastructure assets — not at inflated stock market valuations that include:
- Future profit expectations
- Speculative premiums
- Debt that private owners loaded onto the companies

Market capitalisation ≠ asset value. We pay for the assets, not for the privilege of extinguishing shareholders' future profit expectations. Legal precedent for this exists in infrastructure acquisition law.

## Mechanism Two: Distressed Asset Opportunity

Several water companies are currently in financial difficulty:
- Thames Water: approximately £19 billion in debt, effectively insolvent without government intervention
- Southern Water: ongoing regulatory enforcement, significant financial strain
- Yorkshire Water: under special measures

Distressed acquisition through administration costs a fraction of market rate. The private sector's failure is not a tragedy for nationalisation — it is an opportunity. We acquire failing companies at administration prices, not market prices. The government is already the de facto backstop for these companies. Formalising that is cheaper than pretending it isn't true.

## Mechanism Three: Long-Term Public Bond Financing

Capital acquisition is financed through long-term public infrastructure bonds — not from the revenue budget.

- Infrastructure bonds issued over 10-30 year terms
- Interest rates at government borrowing rates — lower than any private company can borrow
- Bond repayments funded by the revenues of the acquired assets themselves
- The asset pays for its own acquisition

**Example:** If British Water acquires an asset with an annual revenue of £2 billion, and acquisition bonds cost £500 million per year in repayments, the asset is self-financing within its own revenue. The public has acquired an asset that generates net surplus from year one.

## Mechanism Four: Phased Acquisition

Not everything at once. Sequenced by:
1. **Urgency:** Water first — several companies near collapse, infrastructure deteriorating, public health implications
2. **Financial distress opportunity:** Companies in difficulty acquired at lower cost
3. **Operational readiness:** British Water must be legally established and operationally ready before transfer
4. **Parliamentary capacity:** Major nationalisation legislation requires Parliamentary time — sequenced to avoid legislative gridlock

**Timeline:**
- Year 1: British Water legislation; Water company transition begins (distressed companies first)
- Year 2-3: British Grid legislation; energy network transition begins
- Year 3-5: Remaining water transition; energy transition continues
- Year 5-7: Full water and energy operational under public management
- Year 5+: Rail infrastructure (largely already public via Network Rail — completion and integration)
- Year 7-10: Telecommunications backbone (most complex legally due to existing market structure)

# BRITISH OIL — A DIFFERENT MODEL

Oil is different from water and energy networks. It is not a natural monopoly — multiple companies can extract from different fields. And Britain's North Sea is past peak production.

## The Norwegian Template

Norway's Equinor (formerly Statoil) is 67% government-owned. It operates commercially, competes internationally, and funds Norway's sovereign wealth fund with its profits.

Norway did not nationalise its oil industry. It created a state company with a majority government stake that operates alongside private extractors — but government ownership ensures profit flows primarily to Norwegian citizens rather than exclusively to private shareholders.

## The British Model

**British Petroleum plc (new):**
- New state-owned oil company with majority government stake (51%+)
- Operates alongside existing private extractors — we are not compulsorily acquiring BP, Shell, or Equinor's existing operations
- British Petroleum plc bids for new North Sea licences and existing licences that come up for renewal
- Over time, the government stake in British oil production grows as licences roll over
- Existing private operations continue under their existing licences — this is not retrospective expropriation

**Additional measure:**
- Windfall tax reform: existing private extractors pay a windfall tax on excess profits above a defined threshold
- Revenue from windfall tax and British Petroleum plc operations: to the Sovereign Wealth Fund

**Why not full nationalisation of existing operations:**
International investment law protects existing licence holders against expropriation without full market compensation. Full nationalisation of existing BP/Shell operations would require either enormous compensation payments or international arbitration. The licence rollover model achieves the same long-term goal without that legal and financial exposure.

# WHAT NATIONALISATION FIXES SPECIFICALLY

## Water Contamination

**Current:** Water company → internal investigation → company statement → Ofwat investigation → regulatory notice → possible fine (absorbed as cost of doing business) → shareholders still paid dividends

**Under British Water:** CEO of British Water answers to Parliament. Contamination triggers immediate public inquiry with legal force. No private shareholders to protect. No incentive to minimise disclosure. Full accountability to the public, whose water it is.

The Thames Water sewage scandal — 300,000 sewage dumps into British waterways over three years — becomes structurally impossible. Not because people are better, but because the incentive structure that made it profitable to dump sewage rather than fix pipes is removed.

## Leaking Pipes

Britain loses approximately 3 billion litres of water daily through leaking infrastructure. Under private ownership, fixing leaks costs money that reduces dividends. Under public ownership, fixing leaks reduces costs and improves service — the incentive is aligned with the public interest for the first time.

## Energy Price Dependency

British energy prices are set by global markets even when the energy is generated in Britain. Public ownership of the grid and generation does not eliminate global price influences but substantially reduces exposure — particularly as Britain's renewable generation grows. Energy generated by British wind and stored in British infrastructure and sold to British homes through British Grid is not priced by LNG spot markets.

## Rail Fares

Britain has the most expensive rail fares in Europe. Operators under the franchise system extract profit when times are good and return franchises to the government when times are bad. The privatisation of rail operations has produced: higher fares, inconsistent service, and operators subsidised by the public while paying shareholders from good years. British Rail Operations under Tier Two returns to the model where profit, if generated, goes to British citizens as shareholders or to reinvestment in the service.

# WHAT NATIONALISATION DOES NOT FIX

We are honest about limits:

- Nationalisation does not eliminate management failure. Public bodies can be badly managed. The difference is that badly managed public bodies are directly accountable to Parliament, not shielded by corporate governance.
- Nationalisation does not eliminate infrastructure maintenance costs. The pipes still need fixing. The grid still needs upgrading. These costs remain — but the incentive to fix them is now aligned with the public interest.
- Nationalisation does not solve the UK's energy generation capacity gap immediately. Building generation takes years under any ownership model.
- Nationalisation at Tier Two does not eliminate all profit motive — citizen shareholders have an interest in efficiency. This is a feature, not a bug.

# THE TRADE LAW QUESTION

**Will nationalisation violate international trade agreements?**

Short answer: No, for infrastructure. Requires care for operating companies.

**The legal position:**
- Public ownership of infrastructure (Tier One) is explicitly permitted under WTO rules, CPTPP, and every trade agreement Britain has signed. Every WTO member has publicly owned infrastructure.
- Acquisition of operating companies (Tier Two) must be conducted at fair assessed value to satisfy investment treaty obligations — this is what the RAB model does.
- The licence rollover model for oil avoids expropriation claims entirely.

**Our commitment:** Legal opinions from leading international trade law counsel will be published before any nationalisation legislation is introduced. We will legislate within international law, not outside it.

# THE 1970s COMPARISON — AND WHY IT DOESN'T APPLY

**The 1970s nationalised industries failed because:**
- Political interference in operational decisions — ministers decided what to produce and where
- Union capture of operational management — strikes became political rather than industrial
- Soft budget constraints — losses were automatically covered by the Treasury, removing all efficiency incentive
- No competition at any level — the state operated everything from production to retail

**The Britain Forward model avoids all of these:**
- Tier One (infrastructure): run by independent public bodies, not ministerial direction
- Tier Two (operations): 49% citizen shareholders create accountability; competition exists between regions
- Tier Three (services): fully private and competitive
- Hard budget constraints at Tier Two — citizen shareholders lose value if the company is badly managed
- Independent delivery boards insulated from political interference

**The honest comparison is not British Leyland. It is:**
- Norwegian Equinor — commercially operated, internationally competitive, government-majority-owned
- French EDF — publicly owned, reliable, provides France with cheap nuclear energy
- German Deutsche Bahn — publicly owned rail network, competitive freight operations
- Scottish Water — publicly owned, lower bills than equivalent English private companies, lower leakage rates

Britain is the outlier in not having public ownership of core infrastructure. We are returning to the international norm.
