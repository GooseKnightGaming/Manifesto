# ADDITION ONE: SUBSIDISED PUBLIC TRANSPORT
## Pillar Three — National Infrastructure

### The Problem

Public transport in Britain is among the most expensive in Europe. Bus fares have risen faster than inflation for decades while services have been cut. Rail fares price ordinary people off trains. The result: car dependency is not always a choice — it is frequently the only affordable option.

This creates a cascade of problems:
- Road congestion, because cars are the only viable option for many journeys
- Air pollution, concentrated in urban areas with the highest traffic density
- Social exclusion, for people who cannot drive or cannot afford a car
- Carbon emissions, from a transport sector that has barely decarbonised

The argument for public transport subsidy is not ideological. It is straightforwardly economic: a bus carrying 50 people does less road damage, produces less pollution, and uses less road space than 50 cars. Subsidising the bus benefits everyone — including the drivers stuck in less traffic behind it.

### The Policy

**Buses — Free or Heavily Subsidised:**

Buses are the backbone of public transport for most British people outside London and major cities. They have been progressively defunded, deregulated, and reduced until many routes no longer exist and others run only at hours when they serve no practical purpose.

- Bus networks renationalised or brought under municipal control — operated as public services, not commercial enterprises
- Urban and suburban buses: free at point of use, or heavily subsidised (maximum fare capped at a level that does not deter use)
- Rural buses: free at point of use — these serve people with no viable alternative
- Frequency: minimum service standards for all routes serving inhabited areas — a bus every 30 minutes during peak hours is a baseline, not an aspiration
- Night services: restored in towns and cities where they have been cut — night-time economy workers need to get home

**The Luxembourg Precedent:** Luxembourg made all public transport free in 2020. Ridership increased significantly. Car use on affected routes decreased. The cost was modest relative to the economic and environmental benefits.

**Trams and Light Rail:**

- Tram networks extended in cities with existing systems: Manchester, Edinburgh, Nottingham, Sheffield
- New tram and light rail systems assessed for cities with sufficient population density: Leeds, Bristol, other major urban areas
- Capital costs funded through long-term bonds — self-funding from reduced road infrastructure costs and congestion savings
- Free or heavily subsidised fares — same principle as buses

**Urban Rail:**

- Commuter rail services within major city regions: fares capped and subsidised
- Integration with bus and tram networks — one ticket, one network, genuine joined-up public transport
- Oyster-style integrated ticketing extended beyond London to all major city regions

**Funding the Subsidy:**

Public transport subsidy is not a cost — it is an investment with measurable returns:
- Reduced road maintenance costs (buses damage roads less than equivalent car journeys)
- Reduced congestion costs (economic cost of congestion in British cities is estimated in billions annually)
- Reduced air pollution health costs (NHS treatment for pollution-related conditions)
- Increased urban productivity (people on public transport can work; people stuck in traffic cannot)
- Reduced carbon emissions from transport sector

The subsidy is funded from: road pricing revenues (hypothecated to transport), reduced car infrastructure costs, and general taxation — justified by the economic returns.

# ADDITION TWO: WALKABLE CITIES
## Pillar Three — National Infrastructure / Pillar Four — Strong Communities

### The 15-Minute City Principle

The most liveable cities in the world — Copenhagen, Amsterdam, Vienna, Melbourne — share a characteristic: most daily needs are accessible within 15 minutes on foot. Schools, shops, healthcare, parks, work, leisure — arranged so that a car is optional, not essential.

Britain's cities were largely designed around cars in the 20th century, or inherited Victorian street patterns that were never adapted for the scale of modern traffic. The result: town centres hollowed out, high streets declined, people dependent on cars for journeys that should be walkable.

Britain Forward adopts the **15-Minute City** as the design standard for all new development and urban renewal.

### The Policy

**New Cities and New Developments — Designed for Walking:**

- Maximum distances from residential areas to: primary school (10-minute walk), GP surgery (10-minute walk), green space (5-minute walk), supermarket or shops (10-minute walk)
- Street design prioritises pedestrian movement — not car throughput
- Ground floor uses: shops, cafes, services, not car parks or blank walls
- Continuous, well-lit, accessible pavement networks — no gaps, no barriers, no shared surfaces where separate surfaces are possible
- Street furniture that serves pedestrians: benches, shelters, public toilets (see Dignity Infrastructure)

**Existing City Centres — Pedestrianisation:**

- Town and city centre pedestrianisation expanded — more streets permanently car-free
- Shared space (where pedestrians and cars mix with no kerb separation): not the default; used only where evidence supports it — evidence on shared space for people with visual impairments is clear that kerbs matter
- Pavement quality: national minimum standard; uneven, broken, or blocked pavements are not acceptable
- Crossings: sufficient, frequent, with appropriate signal timing — not timed for car throughput at the expense of pedestrians

**Accessibility — Walking for Everyone:**

Walkability is only meaningful if everyone can walk safely. The policy covers:
- Wheelchair and mobility scooter accessibility throughout pedestrian networks
- Tactile paving and audible signals for people with visual impairments — maintained to standard
- Dropped kerbs at all crossings — not just most crossings
- Pavement parking: enforcement substantially strengthened — pavements are for pedestrians, not car storage

**Night-Time Walkability:**

Cities should be safe to walk in at night. This requires:
- Adequate street lighting — not decorative, functional
- Active frontages on key pedestrian routes at night — not dead shop fronts
- Safe crossing points on major roads, available at night not just daytime
- This connects to the night bus policy — you can only walk home if it's safe to do so

**The Health Argument:**

Walkable cities produce measurably healthier populations. People who walk for transport get incidental exercise without needing to "go to the gym." The NHS savings from reduced obesity, cardiovascular disease, and mental health conditions in more walkable cities are documented and substantial. Walkability is health policy as much as transport policy.

# ADDITION THREE: BIKEABLE CITIES
## Pillar Three — National Infrastructure

### The Dutch Lesson

The Netherlands has cycling rates that Britain's cyclists can only dream of. 27% of all journeys in the Netherlands are made by bicycle. In Britain: approximately 2%.

This is not because Dutch people are more virtuous or more athletic. It is because Dutch cities were deliberately redesigned for cycling from the 1970s onward, following a series of policy decisions that prioritised cyclists over cars. The infrastructure followed the political will.

Britain has the political will — cycling is broadly popular, and surveys consistently show large numbers of people would cycle more if they felt safe. What Britain lacks is the infrastructure.

**Why People Don't Cycle in Britain:**

The primary reason is not laziness. It is fear. Cycling on British roads, mixed with fast-moving traffic in narrow lanes with poor driver behaviour and no physical protection, is genuinely dangerous. People who would happily cycle on a protected lane will not cycle on a busy A-road with lorries passing at 40mph.

The solution is physical separation, not appeals to drivers to be nicer.

### The Policy

**Protected Cycle Lanes — Not Painted Lines:**

- Dedicated, physically separated cycle lanes on all urban arterial roads — separated by a physical barrier (raised kerb, planters, or parking buffer), not just a painted line
- Painted lines are not cycle infrastructure; they are a gesture toward cycle infrastructure
- Minimum lane width: 1.5 metres each direction — wide enough for a cargo bike or a cyclist with panniers
- Continuous routes: a cycle network is only useful if it is continuous; a lane that disappears at every junction is not a lane

**Safe Junctions:**

Junctions are where cyclists are most at risk. The Dutch solution: separate cycling phases at traffic lights, where cyclists have their own green phase before or after vehicle traffic. This eliminates the most common collision type (vehicles turning across cyclists).

- Protected junction design at all major urban junctions with cycle infrastructure
- Cycle-specific signals: separate phase; clear markings
- Advanced stop lines at all other junctions — cyclists wait in front of traffic, not alongside it

**Cycle Parking:**

- Secure, covered cycle parking at all public transport stops, stations, and major public buildings
- Cycle parking at workplaces: building regulations updated to require provision proportionate to building occupancy
- Cargo bike parking and charging at key urban locations — cargo bikes replace car trips for deliveries and family transport when secure parking is available

**Bike-Share Schemes:**

- Publicly funded or subsidised bike-share schemes in all cities above a defined population threshold
- E-bike options included — e-bikes extend the viable cycling distance and make cycling accessible to people who could not otherwise manage it
- Integration with public transport accounts — one payment, bike and bus

**E-Bike Subsidy:**

- Subsidised e-bike purchase for households below a defined income threshold — modelled on the successful schemes in France and Germany
- E-bikes make cycling viable for: commuting distances above 5 miles, hilly terrain, people returning to cycling after injury or age-related fitness decline, people carrying children or cargo

**Rural Cycling:**

Rural cycling is different from urban cycling — distances are longer, roads are narrower, and the dangers are different. The policy:
- Rural road speed limit review: 60mph limit inappropriate for many narrow rural roads where cyclists and cars share space; evidence-based review of appropriate limits
- Signage: rural cycle route signage improved and standardised
- Off-road cycle paths alongside major rural A-roads where traffic volume and speed make on-road cycling dangerous

**The Health, Environmental, and Economic Case:**

- Health: cycling commuters have substantially better health outcomes than car commuters; NHS savings are documented
- Environment: cycling is zero-emission for the journey itself; significant if it replaces car trips
- Economic: cycling infrastructure is dramatically cheaper per person moved than road or rail infrastructure; cyclists spend more in local shops than car drivers (counter-intuitive but consistently documented — parking requirements mean car-oriented development has less retail per square mile)
- Road capacity: a cycle lane replacing a car lane carries more people per hour than the car lane did

# HOW THESE THREE CONNECT

**Public transport, walkability, and cycling are not three separate transport policies. They are one integrated alternative to car dependency.**

The logic:
- You walk to the bus stop because the street is safe and pleasant to walk
- You take the bus because it is free or cheap and comes frequently
- You cycle for shorter journeys because there is a safe lane to cycle in
- For longer journeys or bad weather, public transport covers it
- The car is optional rather than essential for most daily journeys
- Fewer cars on the road makes the car journeys that remain easier and faster

**For existing car owners:** this is not an attack on driving. It is building genuine alternatives so that driving is a choice, not a compulsion. Traffic jams are not caused by people who love being stuck in them — they are caused by the absence of viable alternatives.

**For the green transition:** the transport sector is one of the largest contributors to British carbon emissions and one of the slowest to decarbonise. Reducing car journeys through genuine alternatives is faster and cheaper than waiting for the entire car fleet to go electric.
