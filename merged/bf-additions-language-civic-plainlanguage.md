# PART ONE: THE BASIC LANGUAGE COMPETENCY STANDARD

## The Principle

Functional communication in a native British language — English, Welsh, Scottish Gaelic, Irish, Scots, Cornish — is a reasonable expectation of participation in British civic and economic life.

This is not an Oxonian standard. It is not about accent, dialect, regional variation, or stylistic preference. It is about basic functional literacy and communication: the ability to make yourself understood in writing and speech at a level sufficient to navigate daily life, employment, civic participation, and legal processes.

This standard applies to everyone — because the alternative, a standard that applies only to immigrants, would be both discriminatory and immediately circumventable.

## The Political Reality — Stated Honestly

A universal language competency standard will be presented by opponents as targeting immigrants. It does not target immigrants. It targets functional illiteracy wherever it exists.

The people who write "we want are cuntry back" and "there stealing are jobs" have failed the test they are demanding others pass. A universal standard, honestly applied, exposes this. An immigrant who arrives, learns English to a functional standard, and passes the test is demonstrably more capable of the civic participation the test is designed to support than a native-born person who cannot spell "country."

The test is the point. The universality is the point. The equal application is the point.

## What the Test Covers

**Not tested:**
- Spelling perfection
- Grammar to academic standard
- Vocabulary breadth
- Formal writing style
- Accent or pronunciation (for spoken elements)
- Regional dialect or vernacular expression

**Tested — basic functional communication only:**
- Reading and understanding a short plain-English text (e.g. a letter from a GP surgery, a council notice, a job advertisement)
- Writing a short response that communicates a clear meaning (e.g. a simple letter, a form, a basic message)
- Understanding and following simple spoken instructions
- Making yourself understood in spoken communication on everyday topics

**The standard:** Can this person navigate everyday British life — access healthcare, understand a contract, communicate with an employer, participate in a civic process — using language? Not perfectly. Functionally.

## Language Options

The test is available in:
- English
- Welsh
- Scottish Gaelic
- Irish (in Northern Ireland contexts)
- Scots (as a distinct language, not merely dialect)
- Cornish (where the individual demonstrates it as their primary language)
- British Sign Language (BSL) — full equivalence; BSL is a recognised British language

Choosing a language other than English is not a disadvantage. The standard is functional communication in a native British language, not English specifically.

## Accommodations — Non-Negotiable

The test must never become a tool of exclusion for people with genuine communication differences:

**Dyslexia:**
- Extended time
- Oral alternatives to written components
- Screen reader and text-to-speech support
- Alternative question formats
- Spelling errors that don't affect comprehension are not penalised — the test measures communication, not spelling

**Other Specific Learning Differences (dyscalculia where relevant, dysgraphia, processing speed differences):**
- Format adaptations as above
- Reader/scribe support available
- Extended time

**Cognitive Impairment — Genuine:**
- Medical assessment pathway: certified cognitive impairment that genuinely prevents functional communication at the basic threshold — formally assessed by an independent medical panel, not self-declared
- Exemption or adapted assessment for those with genuine impairment
- Support and advocacy provision rather than punitive consequences

**Physical Disability affecting communication:**
- AAC (Augmentative and Alternative Communication) devices accepted
- BSL as equivalent pathway
- Extended time and format adaptations

**Acquired conditions (stroke, brain injury, progressive neurological conditions):**
- Medical assessment pathway
- Rehabilitation support prioritised over punitive consequence
- Reassessment available after rehabilitation

**Recent arrival:**
- Newly arrived residents have a defined period (minimum 24 months) to reach the standard before assessment
- Free language education provided during this period — the state's obligation is to enable, not just to demand

## Consequences — Honest and Constitutional

**This is where the honest limits apply:**

**British citizens — native-born or naturalised:**
Cannot be deported. This is constitutionally absolute — international law, domestic citizenship law, and the ECHR all prohibit the deportation or effective statelessness of nationals.

The consequence for a citizen who fails:
- Mandatory enrolment in a funded language support programme
- Retest available after programme completion
- No punitive financial consequence beyond the programme requirement
- The political consequence — having demonstrably failed a standard they claim others should meet — is its own result

**Non-citizens with settled status:**
- Fail triggers: mandatory funded language programme; retest required
- Continued failure after programme: application for citizenship refused until standard is met
- Not deportable purely on this basis if lawfully resident — but settlement and citizenship pathway affected

**Non-citizens without settled status (visa holders, recent arrivals, immigration applicants):**
- Language competency standard integrated into immigration assessment (already partly exists)
- Failure of the standard: visa application refused or renewal refused
- Grace period: genuine recent arrivals have time and support before assessment applies
- Deportation for non-citizens who fail and have no right of residence: legally possible and consistent with current immigration law

**Asylum seekers:**
- Asylum assessment not affected by language competency — asylum is a humanitarian process, not a language test
- Language support provided; competency expected within a defined integration period if asylum is granted

## When the Test Is Taken

- **For existing British citizens:** Available voluntarily at any time. Mandatory only for those seeking certain civic positions (see Civic Knowledge section). No retroactive requirement for those who simply continue their lives.
- **For immigrants:** At point of settlement application and naturalisation application — as now, strengthened
- **For young people:** Democratic literacy and language competency built into the school curriculum — the test becomes a natural outcome of education rather than an external imposition

## Administration

- Delivered through libraries, job centres, adult education centres, and online
- Free at point of use — always
- Results available immediately for digital tests; within 14 days for assessed formats
- Retest available with no limit — the goal is competency, not failure
- Independent assessment body — not administered by the Home Office or immigration services; genuinely neutral

# PART TWO: CIVIC KNOWLEDGE — INFORMED PARTICIPATION

## The Honest Problem with Voting Prerequisites

Literacy tests as voting prerequisites have a specific and severe historical record in the United States, where they were used explicitly and systematically to disenfranchise Black voters. The history is not abstract — it was deliberate, sustained, and effective at suppressing democratic participation for millions of people.

Any proposal that links civic knowledge to voting rights will — correctly — be assessed against that history. Britain Forward does not propose a voting prerequisite. Full stop.

**What Britain Forward proposes instead:** Civic knowledge as a naturalisation requirement, a school curriculum outcome, and a voluntary resource for all — building a genuinely informed electorate through education rather than through disenfranchisement.

## Civic Knowledge as a Naturalisation Requirement

Anyone applying for British citizenship demonstrates civic knowledge as part of the naturalisation process.

**What this covers:**
- How Parliament works — what MPs do, how bills become law, what the House of Commons is
- How elections work — how to register, how to vote, how votes are counted, what the result means
- What the major public services are and how to access them
- Basic rights and responsibilities under British law — not legal expertise, basic awareness
- What the devolved institutions are (Holyrood, Senedd, Stormont) and how they relate to Westminster
- How local government works — what councils do, how to contact them

**What this does NOT cover:**
- Party politics — no test question favours or disadvantages any party
- Current political events — not a news quiz
- The "right" answer to contested policy questions — civic knowledge is constitutional and procedural, not ideological

**The test:** Available in multiple languages with the same accommodations as the language competency test. Free. Retakable. Supported by preparation resources.

**The difference from a voting test:** Every British citizen has the right to vote — including those who know nothing about how Parliament works. Civic knowledge is a naturalisation standard and an educational aspiration, not a gatekeeping mechanism for the franchise.

## Civic Knowledge in Schools — Already in the Platform

The platform (section 5.7) already includes mandatory civic education covering how Parliament works, how to vote, how to contact representatives, and how public services function. This section strengthens and connects that existing commitment.

The goal: future generations of British citizens arrive at adulthood with genuine civic knowledge as a natural outcome of their education — not as an imposition, not as a test, but as the normal result of having been educated in Britain.

## Voluntary Civic Knowledge Resource — Open to All

A free, publicly available civic knowledge resource:
- Online and in libraries
- Available in plain English and multiple other languages
- Covering everything in the naturalisation civic knowledge standard and more
- Constantly updated
- Linked to the transparent tax spending information (also in the platform)

The purpose: anyone who wants to be better informed about how Britain works has instant, free access to clear, plain-English information about it. This is the positive version of civic knowledge — enabling, not gatekeeping.

# PART THREE: PLAIN LANGUAGE — A FORMAL LEGAL STANDARD

## The Problem

Government, legal, and public communications in Britain are frequently:
- Written in jargon that ordinary people cannot parse
- Structured to protect the institution rather than inform the reader
- Deliberately vague to preserve bureaucratic flexibility
- So dense that people give up reading and miss information they need
- Or so simplified that important detail is lost and people are misled

This is not neutral. Incomprehensible communications disproportionately affect people with lower literacy, cognitive differences, English as a second language, and less exposure to bureaucratic language through education or profession. It is a form of structural disadvantage that costs people money, rights, and outcomes — every day, invisibly.

## Plain Language as Law — Not Aspiration

Britain Forward introduces a **Plain Language Standard** as a legal requirement for all public communications.

**What this means in practice:**

All communications from any public body — government departments, local councils, NHS trusts, courts, HMRC, DWP, Home Office, regulatory bodies — must meet the Plain Language Standard before issue.

**The Standard:**
- Reading age: accessible to someone reading at approximately age 12 level for standard communications; age 9 level for emergency or safety communications
- Sentence length: average under 20 words; no sentence over 35 words
- Paragraph length: short; one idea per paragraph
- Jargon: defined on first use; avoided wherever plain alternatives exist
- Acronyms: defined on first use; avoided in public-facing communications where possible
- Passive voice: minimised — "we will contact you" not "you will be contacted"
- Active, direct address: "you need to do X by Y date" not "it is required that the recipient comply with X by the relevant date"
- Plain English summaries: any communication over 500 words must include a plain English summary of key actions required
- Next steps: every communication makes clear what the person needs to do, by when, and what happens if they don't

**What this explicitly covers:**
- All letters from government departments
- All online forms and digital services
- All legal notices
- All benefit award and refusal letters
- All tax communications
- All healthcare communications (appointment letters, discharge summaries, treatment plans)
- All court documents intended for members of the public
- All policy documents — including in the two-format Evidence Commitment (already in platform)
- Political communications from publicly funded bodies (not party political materials)

**What is exempt:**
- Internal legal and technical documents between qualified professionals (contracts between lawyers, technical specifications between engineers) — these have legitimate technical language requirements
- Academic publications — though plain English summaries are still required

## The Plain Language Enforcement Body

An independent **Plain Language Commissioner** — a new office with:
- Powers to audit public communications
- Powers to require rewriting before issue
- Powers to fine bodies that systematically fail the standard
- A public reporting function — annual report on Plain Language Standard compliance across public bodies
- Exemption application process for genuinely specialist communications

The Commissioner's decisions are judicially reviewable but not ministerially overridable.

## Plain Language in the Courts

Legal proceedings involving members of the public — criminal courts, family courts, immigration tribunals, benefit appeals — must be conducted in language that participants can understand.

- Every participant in a legal proceeding has the right to a plain-language explanation of what is happening, what is being decided, and what their options are
- Legal aid includes plain-language advocacy — explaining, not just representing
- Judgements that affect members of the public must include a plain-language summary of the decision and its implications
- This does not change the legal standard or precedent value of the full judgment — it adds an accessible layer

## Plain Language and Britain Forward's Own Communications

Britain Forward commits to the same standard for its own communications:
- All policy documents: published in technical AND plain English (already in Evidence Commitment)
- All political materials: written to the Plain Language Standard
- All speeches: plain English; no political convolution; no double-talk; what is meant is what is said
- If something cannot be explained plainly, that is a signal that it has not been thought through clearly enough — not that the audience isn't sophisticated enough

> "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough."
> *The principle, attributed in various forms to Feynman, Einstein, and others — regardless of who said it first, it is right.*

# HOW THESE THREE CONNECT

**Language Competency → Civic Knowledge → Plain Language** form a coherent whole:

1. Everyone is expected to be able to communicate functionally in a British language — and is supported to reach that standard

2. Everyone who becomes a British citizen understands how British democracy works — not as gatekeeping, but as genuine preparation for participation

3. Everything the state communicates back to people is in language they can actually understand — because functional communication is only useful if the state communicates in kind

The state cannot reasonably expect citizens to communicate clearly with it while communicating incomprehensibly at them. These three commitments are reciprocal. Britain Forward commits to all three directions simultaneously.
