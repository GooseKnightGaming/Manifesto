# BRITAIN FORWARD
## POLICY ADDITIONS — Foot Diversity, Gait Bias & Physical Presentation Discrimination
### For Integration into Platform v10

---

# THE ISSUE — STATED PLAINLY

There exists a category of unfairness that sits below the threshold of named, codified discrimination — not because it doesn't exist, but because it has never been loud enough, visible enough, or politically convenient enough to name.

People with Egyptian foot (where the big toe is the longest toe — a configuration common in Britain but significantly underrepresented in arts, sports, swimming, dance, and professional contexts) experience a compounding set of disadvantages that operate through structural and unconscious mechanisms rather than conscious discrimination.

This is not the same as discrimination on the basis of race, sex, or sexuality. It carries no historical injustice at that scale. It is not a protected characteristic and should not be treated as one.

But it is real. And Britain Forward's commitment to evidence-based policy and to identifying unfairness wherever it operates means naming it, commissioning research on it, and addressing the structural factors that create it.

---

# THE MECHANISM — HOW IT WORKS

**Stage One: Footwear designed for a different foot shape**

Most commercial footwear — including professional shoes, sports shoes, dance shoes, pointe shoes, swimming fins, and school shoes — is designed around the assumption that the second toe is equal to or longer than the big toe. A foot where the big toe is longest sits differently in standard footwear: more pressure on the big toe, different load distribution, altered alignment.

This is not a niche problem. If Egyptian foot is genuinely common in Britain — and there is reason to believe it may be significantly underrepresented in visible contexts for reasons explored below — then a large proportion of the population is routinely wearing footwear that does not fit their actual foot geometry.

**Stage Two: Discomfort changes gait**

Poorly fitted footwear changes how people walk. Compensation for pressure, discomfort, or misalignment alters gait in ways that are rarely conscious and rarely noticed by the person experiencing them — but are often unconsciously registered by observers.

**Stage Three: Gait affects perception in professional and social contexts**

This is where well-evidenced research intersects with the specific issue. Studies on interview bias consistently show that observers make rapid, largely unconscious assessments of candidates based on physical presentation — including walk, posture, and bearing. These assessments happen within seconds of a person entering a room and are difficult to override even when interviewers are aware of the bias.

If Egyptian foot creates gait patterns that are unconsciously read as "less confident," "less professional," or simply "different from the norm" — and if those reading those patterns are themselves predominantly people without that foot configuration — the result is systematic disadvantage operating entirely below the surface of conscious decision-making.

**Stage Four: Avoidance of visibility creates underrepresentation**

If people with this foot configuration learn — consciously or not — that their feet provoke comment, discomfort, or disadvantage in visible contexts (swimming, barefoot environments, open-toed shoes, dance), they may systematically avoid those contexts. Over time, this creates underrepresentation in exactly the places where foot shape is most visible: arts, swimming, sports, dance. The underrepresentation then reinforces the impression that this configuration is rare or abnormal — which reinforces the avoidance — which reinforces the underrepresentation.

This is a self-sustaining feedback loop that requires no conscious prejudice to maintain.

# WHERE THIS CONNECTS TO EXISTING POLICY

## 1. Interview Reform — Gait and Physical Bearing Bias

**Existing policy (Platform 5.5):** Banned interview questions; alternative assessment formats; neurodivergent adaptations.

**Addition:**

The interview reform section should explicitly address unconscious physical presentation bias — including gait, walk, and bearing — as a known and documented bias vector.

*Proposed addition to section 5.5:*

> **Physical Presentation and Gait Bias:**
>
> Research on interview bias documents that assessors make rapid, largely unconscious judgements based on how candidates physically present — including walk, posture, and bearing. These assessments are made within seconds of a candidate entering a room, often before any substantive interaction has occurred.
>
> Where physical presentation (including gait) is influenced by factors entirely unrelated to job performance — including structural foot differences, footwear design, disability, chronic pain, or neurological variation — assessments based on it are both unfair and poor predictors of actual job capability.
>
> Britain Forward commits to:
> - Including gait and physical bearing bias in unconscious bias training frameworks for public sector hiring
> - Commissioning the Office for Evidence-Based Policy to research the scope of physical presentation bias in British hiring contexts, including the specific mechanisms by which foot structure and footwear design may influence gait and assessor perception
> - Extending "alternative assessment formats" to explicitly include alternative entry formats — candidates should not be disadvantaged by a walk across a room before an interview has begun
> - Requiring public sector hiring panels to be trained in recognising and discounting physical presentation cues unrelated to role performance

## 2. Consumer Standards — Footwear Design for Foot Diversity

**Existing policy (Platform 2.9):** Right to Repair; consumer standards; British industry standards.

**Addition:**

Footwear design currently assumes a narrow range of foot geometry. This is a consumer standards issue — particularly for professional footwear, school shoes, sports shoes, and medical footwear — where people are effectively required to wear footwear that does not fit their actual foot.

*Proposed addition to consumer standards section:*

> **Footwear Design Standards — Foot Diversity:**
>
> British Standards for footwear — including school shoes, professional footwear, sports shoes, and medically recommended footwear — should reflect the actual range of foot geometries present in the British population.
>
> Britain Forward commits to:
> - Commissioning research through the Office for Evidence-Based Policy on the actual prevalence of different foot configurations in the British population — including Egyptian foot, Morton's toe, and other common variations — to establish a reliable evidence base currently absent from public health and consumer standards literature
> - Reviewing British Standards for footwear design in light of evidence on foot diversity
> - Requiring that publicly procured footwear (school uniforms where shoes are specified, professional uniforms, NHS-recommended footwear) is available in designs accommodating documented foot diversity
> - Encouraging — through the Right to Fit standard (see below) — manufacturers to offer genuine width and toe-box diversity, not just length sizing

> **The Right to Fit:**
>
> Extending the consumer standards principle to footwear: people should be able to access footwear that fits their actual foot, not a notional average foot. Where footwear is effectively mandatory — school, professional, or safety contexts — design must accommodate the actual range of foot shapes, not a single assumed geometry.

## 3. Arts Access — Publicly Funded Institutions and Footwear Requirements

**Existing policy (Platform 4.7):** Arts funding; arts access; after-school arts programmes.

**Addition:**

Dance schools, theatre companies, and arts institutions in receipt of public funding impose specific footwear requirements — pointe shoes, character shoes, professional footwear — that are designed around particular foot geometry assumptions. Where those requirements inadvertently create access barriers for people with common foot configurations, they become a public interest question.

*Proposed addition to arts access policy:*

> **Physical Access Standards in Publicly Funded Arts:**
>
> Arts institutions in receipt of public funding — including dance schools, theatre conservatoires, arts education programmes, and performance companies — should be required to audit their physical and equipment requirements for inadvertent exclusion on the basis of body diversity, including foot geometry.
>
> This does not mean removing artistic standards. It means distinguishing between standards that reflect genuine artistic or technical necessity and standards that reflect assumed norms that may exclude capable participants unnecessarily.
>
> Specifically:
> - Dance footwear requirements in publicly funded training should be assessed for whether they accommodate the actual range of foot configurations in the population
> - Where standard footwear creates documented disadvantage for participants with particular foot structures, adapted alternatives should be available
> - Audition and assessment processes should explicitly exclude physical presentation factors unrelated to artistic capability — a candidate's foot shape is not relevant to their talent
>
> The Office for Evidence-Based Policy will commission research into the prevalence of foot-configuration-related barriers in access to arts education and professional arts training.

## 4. Sports and Swimming Access

**Existing policy (Platform 4.6):** Swimming universal provision; sports and physical activity.

**Addition:**

Swimming fins, specialist sports footwear, and cycling shoes are similarly designed around particular foot geometry assumptions. Where equipment design creates access barriers — particularly in publicly funded sports and swimming programmes — those barriers should be assessed and addressed.

*Proposed addition:*

> **Equipment Design and Physical Diversity in Sport:**
>
> Publicly funded sports programmes and facilities should assess whether standard equipment (including swimming fins, cycling shoes, athletic footwear, and specialist sports equipment) accommodates the actual range of foot configurations in the population.
>
> Where standard equipment creates documented disadvantage for participants with particular foot structures, publicly funded programmes should offer adapted alternatives — not requiring participants to self-fund workarounds for a design gap that is not their responsibility.

## 5. Office for Evidence-Based Policy — Research Commission

**The honest acknowledgement:**

The evidence base specifically linking Egyptian foot prevalence, footwear design, gait bias in professional contexts, and underrepresentation in arts, sports, and professional settings is currently thin. The *mechanisms* are well-evidenced. The *specific application* rests on observed experience rather than published research.

This is exactly the kind of question the Office for Evidence-Based Policy exists to address.

*Proposed commission:*

> **Research Commission: Foot Configuration, Footwear Design, and Structural Disadvantage**
>
> The Office for Evidence-Based Policy is commissioned to investigate:
>
> 1. The actual prevalence of different foot configurations (including Egyptian foot, Morton's toe, and other common variations) in the British population — disaggregated by region, ethnicity, and demographic where relevant
>
> 2. The relationship between foot configuration and standard footwear design — specifically whether common British foot configurations are systematically disadvantaged by current design norms
>
> 3. The documented relationship between gait, physical presentation, and professional and social outcomes — including interview assessment, arts audition, and sports selection contexts
>
> 4. The extent to which footwear-induced gait differences may mediate professional and social outcomes in ways unrelated to actual capability
>
> 5. Representation patterns in visible barefoot contexts (swimming, dance, arts) — whether any foot configurations are statistically underrepresented and, if so, through what mechanisms
>
> Research to be published in full — technical and plain English versions simultaneously — within the first Parliament.
>
> Policy in this area will follow the evidence. If the research confirms the mechanisms described above, Britain Forward will legislate accordingly. If the research does not confirm them, Britain Forward will say so openly and explain what it found instead.

# HOW TO FRAME THIS PUBLICLY

This is not a protected characteristic. It should not be presented as one.

The framing that works:

> "Britain Forward is committed to identifying and addressing unfairness wherever it operates — including the subtle, uncodified, and previously unnamed kinds. We know from direct experience and emerging evidence that foot configuration interacts with footwear design, gait, and unconscious professional bias in ways that disadvantage real people in real situations. We don't yet have the full evidence base. That's why we're commissioning it. If the evidence confirms what experience suggests, we'll act. If it doesn't, we'll say so."

This framing:
- Takes the experience seriously without overclaiming
- Commits to evidence before legislation
- Demonstrates that Britain Forward's evidence commitment applies to *unusual* questions, not just comfortable ones
- Distinguishes clearly from protected characteristic status while still treating the issue with respect
