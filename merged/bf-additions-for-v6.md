# ADDITION ONE: SMALL BUSINESS TAX RELIEF
## Pillar Two — Build the Economy

### The Problem

Small businesses are the backbone of the British economy. They employ the majority of British workers, anchor local high streets, drive regional economic diversity, and provide the seedbed from which larger enterprises grow.

They are also taxed, regulated, and administered in ways designed for large organisations — creating compliance costs that are proportionally far higher for a ten-person company than for a ten-thousand-person one. The result: small businesses spend money on accountants, regulatory compliance, and administration that could otherwise go into staff, equipment, and growth.

### The Policy

**Tiered Corporation Tax by Revenue:**

| Annual Revenue | Corporation Tax Rate |
|---|---|
| Under £500,000 | Substantially reduced rate |
| £500,000 - £5 million | Intermediate rate |
| £5 million - £50 million | Standard rate |
| Over £50 million | Full rate (no reduction) |

*Rates to be confirmed by independent economic modelling — the structure is fixed, the precise percentages follow evidence on optimal growth incentive vs revenue impact.*

**What this achieves:**
- A small business retains more profit to reinvest in staff, equipment, and expansion
- The incentive to grow is built in — moving up the revenue tier is a sign of success, not a penalty trigger
- Local and regional economies benefit — small businesses spend locally; their profits recirculate in their communities

**The Anti-Avoidance Rule — Essential**

Without this, large businesses fragment into artificial small units to claim the lower rate. This is not theoretical — it happens wherever tiered rates exist without robust anti-fragmentation rules.

**Anti-fragmentation requirements:**
- Companies under common ownership or control are assessed together for tax purposes regardless of how they are structured
- The "common control" test covers: shared directors, shared premises, shared supply chains, shared brand, shared beneficial ownership above a defined threshold
- Artificial fragmentation is treated as tax avoidance — penalties apply
- HMRC given enhanced investigatory powers specifically for this area

**Additional Small Business Measures:**

- **Compliance cost reduction:** Single simplified reporting framework for businesses under £500,000 revenue — one return, one set of rules, one point of contact at HMRC
- **Business Rates reform:** Small businesses on high streets receive substantial business rates relief; rate for large commercial landlords and out-of-town retail maintained or increased where appropriate
- **Late payment enforcement:** Large companies paying small suppliers late — currently a chronic and documented problem — face escalating penalty interest; small businesses have a right to expedited payment dispute resolution
- **Procurement access:** Public contracts below a defined threshold reserved for British small businesses — not allowed to be bundled into large contracts that only large businesses can bid for

# ADDITION TWO: BIG BUSINESS — TAXED FAIRLY AND FULLY
## Pillar Two — Build the Economy

### The Principle

Small businesses get relief to grow. Large businesses pay their share — fully, without loopholes, without artificial offshoring of profits.

This is not anti-business. Profitable large businesses are a sign of economic health. It is anti-avoidance: the distinction between a business that succeeds and pays its taxes, and a business that succeeds and structures itself to pay as little tax as possible while using British infrastructure, British workers, and British consumers.

### The Policy

**Corporation Tax — Large Businesses:**
- Large businesses (over £50 million annual revenue) pay the full corporation tax rate — no tiered relief
- Rate to be set at a level that is internationally competitive without enabling systematic profit extraction

**Anti-Avoidance — The Full Suite:**

*Transfer pricing:* Multinational companies selling services or goods between their own subsidiaries at artificially inflated prices to move profit to lower-tax jurisdictions — tightened rules, enhanced HMRC capacity to challenge, burden of proof on the company to demonstrate arm's-length pricing.

*Controlled Foreign Company rules:* British companies routing profits through foreign subsidiaries in low-tax jurisdictions — strengthened CFC rules; profits genuinely attributable to British operations taxed in Britain.

*Thin capitalisation:* Companies loading British subsidiaries with artificial debt to a foreign parent, claiming interest deductions that reduce British taxable profit — interest deductibility capped at a defined percentage of operating profit.

*Digital services:* Companies generating substantial revenue from British users while booking that revenue in low-tax jurisdictions — digital services tax maintained and potentially strengthened, pending international agreement on minimum corporate tax.

**HMRC Investment:**
- HMRC's large business compliance division receives substantial additional resource — enforcement pays for itself many times over
- Every additional pound invested in large business tax compliance has historically returned several pounds in recovered revenue
- Tax compliance as a public good — not a bureaucratic burden but a funding mechanism for everything else

**The Message:**
Britain welcomes successful large businesses. We want them here. We want them employing British people and using British infrastructure. In return, they pay their taxes in Britain — not in Dublin, Luxembourg, or the Cayman Islands. The choice between being welcome and paying taxes here, or not paying taxes and finding Britain a less welcoming place to operate, is a real one.

# ADDITION THREE: FUEL DUTY RELIEF DURING CRISES
## Pillar Two / Pillar Three — Economy / Infrastructure

### The Problem

Fuel prices spike during oil crises, geopolitical disruptions, and supply shocks. These spikes hit working people, rural communities (where car dependency is highest and alternatives are fewest), and small businesses (whose logistics costs are directly tied to fuel prices) hardest and fastest.

The government currently has no formal, automatic mechanism for providing relief during acute price spikes. Relief is announced ad hoc, politically — creating uncertainty and the impression of panic rather than planned response.

### The Policy

**Automatic Fuel Duty Reduction Trigger:**

A formal statutory mechanism:

- A defined fuel price threshold — set by reference to the 36-month rolling average retail price, plus a defined percentage above that average — triggers automatic fuel duty reduction
- Reduction applies for as long as retail prices remain above the trigger threshold
- Reduction is proportionate — the higher prices go above the threshold, the greater the duty reduction, up to a defined maximum
- Reduction is temporary by design — it does not permanently lower fuel duty; when prices return below the trigger, duty returns to its normal level
- The trigger mechanism, threshold, and maximum reduction are set in statute — not by ministerial announcement; not subject to political pressure to trigger early or withdraw late

**Why Automatic:**
- Certainty for businesses and consumers — they know the rule, not the politics
- Removes the "government deciding whether to help" dynamic — the help is structural, not a favour
- Prevents lobbying — the threshold is a number, not a negotiation

**Transition Context:**
As road-embedded charging replaces fuel duty over time, this mechanism adapts — the trigger applies to the equivalent road pricing rate rather than fuel duty specifically. The principle remains: acute cost spikes are cushioned by automatic adjustment.

**Rural and Low-Income Priority:**
During extended fuel duty reductions, priority assistance additionally directed at:
- Rural communities with no viable public transport alternative
- Low-income households where fuel costs represent a higher proportion of income
- Small logistics businesses where fuel is a primary operating cost

**Coal Equivalent:**
Where coal or other transition fuels are still in use during the green energy transition period (see Addition Five), equivalent price shock protection applies to the duty/levy elements of those fuels' costs during acute supply disruptions.

# ADDITION FOUR: INTERNATIONAL GREEN PRESSURE
## Pillar Six — Britain in the World

### The Problem

Britain going green while major economies continue high-carbon production achieves limited global climate impact and potentially disadvantages British industry competitively. A country that invests in expensive green production while importing cheap goods produced using carbon-intensive methods effectively subsidises other countries' carbon emissions.

This is not a reason for Britain not to go green. It is a reason for Britain to use its international influence, trade relationships, and economic leverage to ensure others go green too.

### The Policy

**Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM):**

Imports from countries not meeting agreed minimum carbon standards are subject to a carbon border adjustment — a levy proportionate to the estimated carbon cost of production that is *not* incorporated into the price of the imported good.

- Applies to sectors where British producers face significant carbon costs (energy, steel, cement, chemicals, manufacturing)
- Rate calculated based on: the estimated carbon intensity of production in the exporting country, minus any carbon pricing already paid in that country
- Revenue from CBAM: invested in British green industrial transition and in international green development partnerships
- Compliant with WTO rules — CBAM is explicitly designed within WTO framework; the EU has already implemented a version of this

**The Effect:**
- British green producers are not competitively disadvantaged against foreign carbon-intensive producers
- Foreign producers face a financial incentive to reduce their carbon intensity — British market access improves if they do
- Countries with genuine carbon pricing schemes receive credit — CBAM is not a tariff against all foreign goods, only unpriced carbon

**International Green Pressure — Diplomatic:**

Britain actively builds and leads a coalition of countries committed to:
- Minimum carbon pricing standards
- Phasing out unsubsidised fossil fuel production over agreed timescales
- Transparent carbon accounting

**Mechanisms for pressure:**

*Trade agreements:* All new bilateral trade agreements negotiated by Britain include binding green standards commitments — not aspirational language, binding commitments with enforcement mechanisms.

*Development partnerships:* British international investment (replacing traditional foreign aid — see main platform) prioritises green infrastructure, renewable energy, and sustainable development. Countries that actively undermine international green commitments become lower priority for British partnership investment.

*Naming and accountability:* Britain publishes an annual International Carbon Accountability Report — tracking major economies' commitments and actual performance, published clearly and plainly, reported to Parliament.

*Diplomatic pressure:* In international forums (G7, G20, COP, UN), Britain consistently advocates for binding rather than aspirational commitments, for transparent carbon accounting, and for consequences — not just encouragement — for countries that back out of agreed commitments.

**On Large Emitters Specifically:**

The largest emitters — China, the United States, India, Russia — require specific diplomatic strategies:

*China:* Britain engages bilaterally on green technology partnership — China has significant green energy ambition and substantial green technology capacity. Shared interest in green technology development creates genuine basis for engagement alongside firm pressure on commitments.

*United States:* Alliance relationship provides leverage. British diplomatic position in international forums uses that relationship to advocate for US commitment rather than accepting US backing-out as inevitable. Trade agreement terms can include carbon standard requirements.

*India:* Developing economy status means India's pathway to green energy is longer and requires support as well as pressure. British partnership investment in Indian renewable energy infrastructure creates both goodwill and genuine carbon reduction.

*Russia:* Limited bilateral leverage currently. CBAM is the primary mechanism — Russian goods exported to Britain face carbon adjustment levies unless and until Russian carbon pricing meets agreed standards.

**No Backing Out:**

Britain Forward commits to maintaining international green pressure regardless of short-term diplomatic inconvenience. Countries that announce green commitments and then quietly reverse them will face: public naming in the International Carbon Accountability Report; CBAM at the full unpriced carbon rate; reduced priority in British international partnership investment.

Agreements mean something. Britain will act like they do.

# ADDITION FIVE: TRANSITION FUELS — HONEST SEQUENCING
## Pillar Three — National Infrastructure / Energy

### The Honest Position

Britain is committed to green energy independence. Britain is also honest about timescales.

Renewable energy, nuclear, tidal, hydro, and geothermal cannot replace all current energy demand immediately. The infrastructure takes years to build. The grid needs upgrading to handle distributed renewable generation. Storage technology is improving but not yet fully mature.

During the transition period — which is real, honest, and has a defined endpoint — Britain maintains domestic oil, gas, and coal production where necessary. This is not a contradiction of our green ambitions. It is the honest sequencing of a realistic transition.

Germany provides the cautionary tale: committed to phasing out nuclear prematurely, increased dependence on Russian gas, faced an acute energy crisis when Russian supply was cut. Britain will not repeat that mistake.

### The Policy

**Domestic Fossil Fuel Production — Maintained During Transition**

- British oil and gas fields: production maintained at levels required for British domestic energy security
- British coal: limited production maintained for specific industrial uses where alternatives are not yet viable; phased down as alternatives mature
- No new long-term fossil fuel infrastructure investments — no new coal power stations, no new long-term gas pipelines designed around fossil fuel permanence
- Existing infrastructure: maintained, operated efficiently, and phased out as green alternatives come online — not abandoned prematurely

**British Production for British Need:**
- Domestic fossil fuel production is prioritised for British use — not exported while Britain buys expensively from the international market
- Surplus above British needs: sold internationally at market rate, revenue to Sovereign Wealth Fund
- This is the Norwegian model applied to Britain's remaining fossil fuel capacity

**The Transition Timetable:**
- Not a fixed date for everything to be green — because premature targets that can't be met discredit the commitment
- Instead: milestones for renewable capacity, grid upgrade, storage capacity, and sector-by-sector fossil fuel phase-out
- Each fossil fuel phase-out is triggered when green alternatives can demonstrably meet the relevant demand — not before, not significantly after
- Milestones are public, independently monitored, and reported to Parliament annually

**Worker Transition Support:**
Workers in oil, gas, and coal industries are not abandoned when those industries phase down. This is a Britain Forward commitment:
- Retraining funded and practical — not a leaflet and a website
- Green energy jobs prioritised for former fossil fuel workers where skills transfer
- Regional economic investment in communities dependent on fossil fuel industries — the transition creates new economic activity, not just losses
- No community left behind by the green transition. This is policy, not aspiration.

**Coal — Specific Position:**
Coal is the dirtiest fossil fuel and phases down fastest. However:
- Some steel production requires coking coal for which no green alternative currently exists at scale — limited domestic coking coal production maintained until green steel methods are fully viable
- Coal power generation: no new coal power stations; existing stations operated only as emergency backup capacity during grid transition; phased out as green baseload capacity reaches sufficiency

**The Honest Timeline:**
- This Parliament: aggressive renewable expansion; domestic fossil fuels maintained for security
- 5-10 years: renewable capacity substantially expanded; domestic fossil fuel use declining
- 10-20 years: fossil fuels used only in sectors where green alternatives are not yet viable
- 20+ years: fossil fuel use reduced to minimal residual; Britain primarily green
- These are horizons, not hard deadlines. The trigger is demonstrated green capacity, not a calendar date.

# HOW THESE CONNECT TO EACH OTHER

**Small business relief + big business taxation** form a coherent whole: we're not anti-business, we're pro-fair-contribution. Small businesses building toward scale get relief to grow. Large businesses that have grown and profit enormously contribute fully. The loophole-prevention rules ensure the boundary between the two is real.

**Fuel duty relief + transition fuels** form a coherent energy honesty position: we're committed to green energy, we're honest that the transition takes time, and we protect people from the costs of that transition period — including the price volatility that comes with fossil fuel dependence. These two policies together say: "We know it's not instant. We'll protect you during the gap."

**International green pressure + domestic transition fuels** resolve an apparent contradiction: why should Britain maintain fossil fuel production while pressuring others to go green? Because Britain is maintaining transition fuels for British domestic security during a defined transition — not for permanent expansion, not for export-led fossil fuel growth. Meanwhile, Britain uses its trade and diplomatic relationships to ensure other countries are also genuinely transitioning, not just promising to.

Together, these five additions make the economic and energy sections substantially more complete — and substantially more honest about the real complexity of what Britain Forward is proposing.
