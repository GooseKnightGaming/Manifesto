# WHY THIS DOCUMENT EXISTS

Britain Forward believes in personal liberty. We believe that what consenting adults do in ways that harm nobody else is not the state's business.

We also believe that bad legislation — rushed, underprepared, legally fragile — causes more harm than delayed legislation done properly.

These policies are deferred because:
1. The legal frameworks required to implement them safely do not yet fully exist
2. The operational infrastructure (regulatory bodies, licensing systems, safeguard mechanisms) must be built before legislation passes
3. Public understanding and acceptance, while growing, benefits from honest open debate before a parliamentary vote

These policies are NOT deferred because:
- We don't believe in them
- We are hiding them from voters
- We intend to abandon them after election

**The direction is stated. In writing. Publicly. Before any election.**

---

# CRITERIA FOR ACTION

Each deferred policy has specific, measurable criteria. When those criteria are met, the policy is brought forward within one Parliamentary session — not eventually, not maybe, within one Parliamentary session.

# POLICY ONE: DRUG LAW REFORM

## The Current Problem

Britain's drug classification system — Class A, B, C — is widely criticised by experts, academics, police, and medical professionals as:
- Inconsistent with evidence on actual harm
- Effective at criminalising users while failing to reduce supply
- Counterproductive to public health — criminalisation prevents people seeking help
- Ineffective at disrupting serious organised crime, which profits from illegal markets

The current system does not work. The question is what replaces it.

## The Evidence Base

**Portugal (2001):** Decriminalised personal possession of all drugs. Drug-related deaths fell dramatically. HIV infections among drug users fell. Drug use did not increase significantly. Enforcement resources redirected to trafficking and supply.

**Canada (cannabis, 2018):** Regulated legal cannabis market. Tax revenue generated. Youth access did not increase significantly — illegal dealers don't ask for ID; regulated shops do. Quality controlled, contamination reduced.

**Netherlands:** Tolerated cannabis market for decades. Practical regulation without full legalisation. Mixed results — demonstrates both what works and what doesn't.

**Switzerland (heroin prescription, 1994):** Medically supervised heroin prescription for severe addicts. Crime by addicts fell substantially. Health outcomes improved. Social costs reduced.

## The Policy Framework (When Criteria Are Met)

### Replace Classification with a Harm-Based Regulatory Tier System

**Tier 1 — Low Harm (Cannabis-class substances):**
- Legal to produce, sell, and possess
- Licensed vendors only — age verification mandatory
- No advertising permitted
- Quality standards enforced by regulation
- Taxed — revenue hypothecated to NHS, addiction services, and prevention education
- Possession limits defined — personal use vs supply quantities distinguished

**Tier 2 — Moderate Harm:**
- Controlled access through licensed venues or pharmacies
- Quantity limits per purchase
- Purchaser registration (anonymised for health tracking, not criminalisation)
- Harm reduction information provided at point of sale

**Tier 3 — High Harm:**
- Not freely sold
- Available only through:
  - Medical prescription for addiction treatment
  - Supervised consumption facilities
  - NHS addiction programmes
- No commercial market at this tier

### What Changes for Enforcement

- Personal possession for personal use: decriminalised across all tiers
- Supply outside the licensed system: serious criminal offence, sentences increased
- Trafficking: severe criminal penalties, enforcement resources redirected here from user pursuit
- Exploitation of addicts: treated as serious organised crime

### What Stays Criminal

- Supply to under-18s — enhanced penalties
- Operating outside the licensed system — serious offence
- Trafficking and importation — serious organised crime treatment
- Driving under the influence — equivalent to alcohol law, enforced equivalently

## Criteria for Action

Britain Forward will bring forward drug law reform legislation when:

1. An independent expert panel (medical, legal, policing, public health) publishes a British harm-based index with clear tier assignments for all currently controlled substances
2. A regulatory framework (licensing body, inspection regime, enforcement mechanism) has been fully designed and costed
3. A public health impact assessment has been completed covering addiction services capacity
4. Parliamentary legal counsel has confirmed the legislative pathway is compatible with existing treaty obligations (UN drug conventions — which do allow for decriminalisation and regulated access)
5. Pilot decriminalisation (personal possession only) has run for a minimum of two years with published outcome data

# POLICY TWO: SEX WORK LAW REFORM

## The Current Problem

Sex work in Britain operates in a legal grey area that:
- Leaves workers without legal protection from violence, exploitation, or theft
- Makes it impossible for workers to report crimes against them without self-incrimination risk
- Drives the industry underground — making trafficking victims invisible and harder to identify
- Criminalises vulnerability rather than exploitation
- Consumes policing resources that could target actual traffickers

The current approach does not protect workers. It does not reduce exploitation. It does not help trafficking victims. It does make things worse for the most vulnerable people in the industry.

## The Evidence Base

**New Zealand (2003):** Full decriminalisation. Sex workers gained employment rights, health and safety protections, and ability to report crimes without fear. Trafficking did not increase. Worker health outcomes improved significantly. The New Zealand Prostitution Law Review found overwhelmingly positive outcomes.

**Sweden (1999 — "Nordic Model"):** Criminalises purchase, not sale. Intended to reduce demand. Evidence is contested — workers report being pushed to less safe environments to avoid detection; traffickers adapt rather than leaving. Street-based sex workers particularly disadvantaged.

**Germany (2002):** Full legalisation with registration. Larger commercial venues regulated. Problems identified with scale and enforcement of registration, but worker rights substantially improved for those in the formal system.

**The evidence strongly favours the New Zealand model** for worker safety and trafficking victim identification. Britain Forward will base its framework on this evidence.

## The Policy Framework (When Criteria Are Met)

### Decriminalisation and Regulation

- Sex work between consenting adults: fully legal
- Workers: full employment rights, health and safety protections equivalent to any other worker
- Right to refuse any client, at any time, for any reason — legally protected
- Legal ability to work collectively (two workers sharing premises for safety — currently illegal as "brothel-keeping")
- Access to healthcare without disclosure concerns
- Full ability to report crimes as victims without self-incrimination

### Mandatory Registration for Commercial Operations

- Commercial venues (beyond individual workers): licensed and regulated
- Regular health and safety inspection
- Mandatory anti-trafficking training for venue operators
- Zero-tolerance for any underage involvement — immediate licence revocation plus criminal prosecution

### Enforcement Redirected

- Trafficking: severe criminal offence; enforcement resources substantially increased
- Coercion: criminal offence; workers can report without self-incrimination
- Underage involvement: absolute prohibition; severe penalties
- Policing focus: exploiters, not workers

### Exit Support

- Funded pathways for anyone wishing to leave the industry
- No questions asked, no conditions attached
- Housing support, employment retraining, financial assistance
- Available permanently — not time-limited

### The Taxation Question

Legal sex work is taxable income, like any other self-employment. Workers in the formal system pay tax and national insurance and receive the associated entitlements. This is not a revenue measure — it is a consequence of legal status as a worker.

## Criteria for Action

Britain Forward will bring forward sex work law reform when:

1. A British evidence review of New Zealand's 20+ years of operation has been completed and published
2. A regulatory framework (licensing body, inspection regime, trafficking identification protocols) has been fully designed
3. The Crown Prosecution Service and National Crime Agency have confirmed operational readiness for redirected enforcement
4. Worker representative organisations have been formally consulted and their input incorporated
5. A public health framework for worker healthcare has been designed with NHS England

# POLICY THREE: RELATIONSHIP LAW REFORM

## The Current Situation

British law recognises marriage between two people and civil partnership between two people.

Britain Forward believes the state should recognise the full range of committed adult relationships — provided all parties are consenting adults and full legal frameworks exist to protect everyone involved.

## Why This Matters

Multi-partner relationships exist. They are not recognised by law. The result: people in committed polyamorous relationships have no legal protection for:
- Property rights if a partner dies
- Next-of-kin recognition in medical emergencies
- Parental responsibility clarity
- Financial protections if the relationship ends

The law does not prevent these relationships. It simply refuses to recognise or protect them — leaving the people in them vulnerable.

## The Policy Framework (When Criteria Are Met)

### Registered Multi-Partner Unions

Legal recognition of committed relationships involving more than two consenting adults.

**Requirements for registration:**
- All parties: consenting adults, no coercion (independently verified)
- Independent legal advice for all parties before registration
- Full financial disclosure between partners
- Cooling-off period (minimum 90 days) before registration is finalised
- Legal frameworks for: property ownership, parental responsibility, inheritance, dissolution, partial exit (one partner leaving a continuing multi-partner union)

**Enhanced safeguards:**
- Where significant age disparity exists: additional independent assessment
- Where financial dependency exists: additional independent assessment
- Regular reaffirmation of consent (every five years, not just at registration)
- Strong penalties for coercion — treated as domestic abuse

**What this does NOT do:**
- Create any obligation to enter such arrangements
- Change the default: standard marriage and civil partnership remain the norm
- Override religious institutions — which remain free to recognise or not recognise any arrangement they choose

### Common-Law Partnership Recognition

Recognition of cohabiting partnerships after a defined period (proposed: three years), providing baseline legal protections:
- Property rights on separation
- Next-of-kin recognition
- Inheritance rights if no will exists

This exists in many jurisdictions and protects particularly women who have cohabited for years but have no legal standing when the relationship ends.

## Criteria for Action

1. Full legal framework for multi-partner unions designed and reviewed by Law Commission
2. Family law practitioners formally consulted
3. Safeguarding organisations formally consulted — particularly on coercion risk
4. Tax and benefits implications fully assessed and resolved
5. Dissolution and partial exit mechanisms legally tested

# POLICY FOUR: PERSONAL LIBERTY AND DECRIMINALISATION

## The Principle

What consenting adults do in ways that harm nobody else is not the state's business.

This covers various decriminalisations that are not yet full policy but reflect the party's philosophical position.

## Non-Sexual Public Nudity

Non-sexual public nudity is currently treated inconsistently in British law — not explicitly illegal in most contexts, but prosecutable under public order legislation.

Britain Forward's position: Non-sexual public nudity by consenting adults in appropriate contexts should not be a criminal matter. Reasonable context restrictions apply — designated areas, not near schools, not imposed on unwilling observers in enclosed spaces.

This is a low-priority legislative item. It requires:
- Clear legal definition of "non-sexual" in this context
- Clear designation of appropriate contexts
- Maintained protections for contexts where nudity would be genuinely inappropriate

When the legislative bandwidth exists and the framework is prepared, this will be brought forward.
