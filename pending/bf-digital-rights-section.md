# WHAT THIS SECTION COVERS

The internet is now as essential to daily life as roads, electricity, and water. But unlike those utilities, the digital world currently operates with almost no enforceable consumer protections against deliberate manipulation.

Companies spend millions engineering interfaces specifically designed to trick users into decisions they would not make if given a genuine choice. They track behaviour without meaningful consent. They make it easy to sign up and deliberately difficult to leave. They present "options" that are not really options.

This is not accidental. It is deliberate design. And it ends.

Britain Forward introduces a comprehensive **Digital Rights Framework** covering:

1. Cookie and tracking consent
2. Digital advertising standards
3. Dark patterns — deceptive interface design
4. Algorithmic transparency
5. Data rights
6. Children's digital protections
7. The right to disconnect
8. Digital infrastructure (broadband — already in main platform; referenced here)

# PART ONE: COOKIE AND TRACKING CONSENT

## The Problem

Cookie consent banners — the popups asking whether you agree to tracking — are currently almost universally designed to maximise the number of people who click "Accept All."

The techniques used:

- **"Accept All" given a prominent, brightly coloured button** — "Necessary Only" given a small, grey, hard-to-find alternative
- **Pre-ticked checkboxes** — consent is assumed unless the user actively unticks each box
- **Pre-set slider buttons** — sliders are positioned at "On" or "I Accept" by default
- **Multi-step rejection** — accepting all cookies takes one click; rejecting non-essential cookies requires navigating through multiple screens
- **Vague categories** — "partners and third parties" describes nothing; the user cannot make an informed decision about who they are consenting to
- **Consent walls** — you cannot use the service at all unless you accept all cookies, even where no genuine free alternative exists

None of this is consent in any meaningful sense. It is manufactured compliance extracted through deliberate friction.

## The Rules

**Rule 1 — Non-essential cookies are opt-in, not opt-out.**

Cookies that are not strictly necessary for the basic function of the website or application must be presented as opt-in. The default state — the state that applies if the user does nothing — must be: non-essential cookies not active.

*What counts as strictly necessary:* Session cookies that keep a user logged in. Shopping cart cookies. Cookies that remember language preference. Security cookies that prevent fraud.

*What does not count as strictly necessary:* Advertising tracking. Behavioural analytics. Third-party data sharing. Social media tracking pixels. Performance analytics beyond what is needed to keep the site running.

**Rule 2 — Checkboxes must be empty by default.**

Where cookie consent is presented via checkboxes — one for each category of cookie — every checkbox must begin unchecked. The user ticks the boxes they consent to. No category is pre-selected.

**Rule 3 — Slider buttons must be set to "Off" or "No" by default.**

Where cookie consent is presented via toggle sliders, every slider must begin in the off or declined position. The user activates the categories they consent to.

**Rule 4 — "Necessary Cookies Only" must be more prominent than "Accept All Cookies."**

The button, link, or option that rejects non-essential cookies must be:
- At least as large as the "Accept All" button
- At least as visually prominent — same colour contrast, same font size, same visual weight
- Positioned at least as accessibly — not buried, not requiring scrolling to reach, not displayed in a lighter or greyer colour

Specifically prohibited: presenting "Accept All" as a filled, coloured button while presenting "Necessary Only" as a text link, a grey outline button, or a hyperlink in smaller text.

**Rule 5 — Rejection must take no more steps than acceptance.**

If accepting all cookies takes one click, rejecting all non-essential cookies must take no more than one click. If acceptance is achieved through a single button, a single button equivalent must exist for rejection.

Multi-step rejection processes — "click here, then here, then deselect each category individually, then confirm" — where acceptance is one click, are prohibited.

**Rule 6 — Categories must be named honestly.**

Cookie categories must describe in plain English what they actually do and who receives the data:

*Permitted:* "Advertising cookies — these allow [Company Name] and its advertising partners to show you adverts based on your browsing history."

*Prohibited:* "Partners and third parties." "Enhanced experience." "Personalisation." — where these terms obscure what is actually happening.

**Rule 7 — Consent must be freely given.**

A consent wall — a requirement to accept non-essential cookies in order to access a service, where no genuine free alternative exists — is prohibited for services that are:
- Publicly funded
- Providing essential services (banking, healthcare, utilities, government)
- Dominant in their market to the degree that refusal effectively denies the user access to a category of service

Note: a service may offer a paid, cookie-free version alongside a free, cookie-supported version — this is a genuine choice. The prohibition is on forcing cookie acceptance as the only option for accessing the service at all.

**Rule 8 — Consent must be renewable and withdrawable.**

Users must be able to change their consent preferences at any time, as easily as they gave consent in the first place. A "manage cookies" option must be permanently accessible — not just presented once at first visit.

Withdrawing consent must take effect immediately. Data collected under consent that has since been withdrawn must not be used for new purposes.

# PART TWO: DIGITAL ADVERTISING STANDARDS

## The Problem

Digital adverts — particularly in free mobile games and ad-supported apps — routinely use:

- **Fake exit buttons** — appear to close the advert but open the advertised product instead
- **Invisible or absent exit buttons** — the countdown ends but the exit button never appears, or disappears within a second
- **Undersized exit buttons** — rendered too small for a human finger to tap reliably without accidentally activating the advert
- **Adjacency traps** — exit button positioned immediately adjacent to the advert's call-to-action, engineering accidental clicks
- **False countdowns** — the countdown resets or extends when the user interacts with the advert

None of this is accidental. Average adult fingertip covers approximately 57 pixels. An 8-pixel X button is not a genuine exit option — it is an engineered misclick.

## The Rules

**Rule 1 — Exit buttons must appear immediately when the non-skippable period ends.**

Where an advert includes a mandatory viewing period, the exit button must appear immediately and fully upon expiry of that period. It must remain continuously visible and functional until the user closes the advert or the advert ends.

**Rule 2 — Exit buttons must actually exit.**

The exit button must return the user to their previous activity. An exit button that opens a store, browser, download, or any other destination is a fraudulent interface. It is prohibited.

**Rule 3 — Exit buttons must be a minimum size of 44x44 pixels.**

This is the minimum interactive touch target size recommended by Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, Google's Material Design, and WCAG 2.1 accessibility standards. Using a smaller size in an advert is a deliberate decision to engineer misclicks. It is prohibited.

The minimum applies to the interactive touch target — a small icon surrounded by adequate invisible touch area is compliant. A small icon with no surrounding touch area is not.

**Rule 4 — Exit buttons must have adequate clearance from advert content.**

Minimum 20 pixels clearance between the exit button's touch target and any clickable advert element. The exit button must be visually distinct from advert content and not camouflaged against the background.

**Rule 5 — Countdown timers must be accurate.**

A countdown timer must reflect the actual remaining non-skippable period. Timers that reset or extend as a result of user interaction (other than deliberate engagement chosen by the user) are prohibited unless the user is clearly informed before the countdown begins.

**Rule 6 — No additional barriers after skip.**

Once a user activates a genuine exit button, they return to their previous activity within one second. "Are you sure?" prompts, additional countdowns, or any other delay mechanism after the user has chosen to skip is prohibited.

**Liability:**

Primary liability: the advertiser. Secondary liability: the platform serving the advert.

A user deceived into clicking an advert through a prohibited dark pattern has not consented to the resulting action. Any purchase, download, or subscription initiated through a deceptive click has no legal force and must be cancellable without penalty at the user's request.

# PART THREE: DARK PATTERNS — DECEPTIVE INTERFACE DESIGN

A dark pattern is a deliberately deceptive interface design that causes users to take actions they would not have taken if given a genuine, unobstructed, honest choice.

Intent to deceive does not need to be proven — the effect is sufficient.

## Prohibited Dark Patterns

**Subscription traps (Roach Motels):**
Cancellation must be as easy as sign-up. If sign-up is online, cancellation must be online. Cancellation cannot require a phone call where sign-up did not. Cancellation cannot require more steps than sign-up. Multi-confirmation screens for cancellation where sign-up was one click: prohibited.

**Hidden costs:**
All mandatory costs must be displayed at the first point where a price is shown. Adding fees, delivery charges, service charges, or any other mandatory cost at a later step in the purchase flow — that was not visible at the start — is prohibited.

**False urgency and false scarcity:**
"Only 2 left!" where inventory is not actually limited. "Offer ends in 10 minutes!" where the timer resets on page reload. Countdown timers on prices that do not actually expire. All prohibited.

**Confirmshaming:**
Labelling the "decline" option with text designed to shame users into accepting — "No thanks, I hate saving money"; "No, I don't want to be healthy" — is prohibited in commercial contexts. The decline option must be neutrally labelled.

**Social proof manipulation:**
Displaying false or misleading social proof — fake reviews, inflated user counts, fabricated testimonials — is already illegal under consumer law. Enforcement is strengthened.

**Disguised adverts:**
Content that is paid advertising must be clearly and prominently labelled as advertising. "Sponsored," "Advertisement," or "Ad" labels must be displayed in the same visual prominence as the content itself — not in 6-point grey text beneath it. This applies to social media, search results, and editorial content.

**In-app purchase pressure mechanics targeting children:**
In applications rated for or marketed to children, the following are prohibited: countdown timers on purchase opportunities; "limited offer" false scarcity on in-app items; social pressure mechanics ("your friends have this item"); purchase confirmation screens that appear identical to non-purchase screens.

# PART FOUR: ALGORITHMIC TRANSPARENCY

## The Problem

Algorithms determine what content people see, in what order, with what frequency. These decisions have profound effects on what people believe, how they feel, and how they behave — and they are made by systems with no democratic accountability, no obligation to explain themselves, and financial incentives that actively reward content that generates strong emotional responses regardless of whether that content is true.

## The Rules

**Platforms must explain how their recommendation algorithms work** — in plain English, to users, on request. Not in a terms-of-service document. In plain English.

**Platforms must offer a chronological feed option.** Users must be able to choose to see content in the order it was posted, without algorithmic curation. This must be genuinely accessible — not buried in settings, not reset at each login.

**Amplification of content that platforms know to be false** — where internal review has flagged it as false or misleading — is prohibited where that amplification is the result of recommendation algorithm choices, not organic user sharing.

**Advertising targeting based on inferred sensitive characteristics** — inferred mental health status, political beliefs, religious beliefs, sexual orientation, immigration status — is prohibited. Advertising can be targeted by age, location, and declared interests. It cannot be targeted by characteristics inferred from behaviour that the user has not explicitly disclosed.

**Annual transparency reports:** Platforms above a defined reach threshold must publish annual transparency reports covering: content removed and why; accounts suspended and why; algorithmic changes made and their measured effect on content distribution; advertising targeting categories used.

# PART FIVE: DATA RIGHTS

**The right to your own data:**

Any person has the right to receive, in a machine-readable format, all data held about them by any platform or service. The request must be fulfilled within 30 days. This is already law under UK GDPR — enforcement is strengthened and penalties increased for non-compliance.

**The right to deletion:**

Any person has the right to request deletion of their data. Deletion must be genuine — not archiving under a different label. Verification that deletion has occurred must be provided. Right to deletion already exists — enforcement strengthened.

**The right to explanation:**

Where an automated decision has a significant effect on a person — credit refusal, content removal, account suspension, insurance pricing, employment screening — that person has the right to a plain English explanation of how the decision was reached. "The algorithm decided" is not an explanation. The factors considered and their weights must be disclosed.

**The right to correction:**

Where data held about a person is inaccurate, they have the right to have it corrected. Where that inaccurate data has been used to make decisions, those decisions must be reviewed.

**Data minimisation — enforced:**

Companies may only collect data that is genuinely necessary for the service they are providing. "We might find this useful later" is not a justification. The principle: collect what you need, delete what you don't, keep it only as long as you need it.

# PART SIX: CHILDREN'S DIGITAL PROTECTIONS

Children deserve stronger protections in digital environments than adults. They are more susceptible to manipulation, less able to evaluate commercial intent, and more vulnerable to the long-term effects of algorithmically curated content.

**Age-appropriate design:**

Platforms and services likely to be accessed by children must implement age-appropriate design — simpler interfaces, no advertising targeting, no algorithmic content amplification, no engagement-maximising mechanics (infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks, notifications designed to create compulsive checking behaviour).

**No advertising targeting to children:**

Any form of behavioural advertising targeting — using data about the user's behaviour to select which advert they see — is prohibited for users under 18.

**No engagement manipulation mechanics for children:**

Streaks, daily login bonuses, social comparison mechanics, push notifications designed to trigger return visits, autoplay, and infinite scroll are prohibited in services primarily used by children.

**Social media minimum age:**

The existing UK social media minimum age framework is supported and enforced. Platforms must implement genuine age verification — not a checkbox.

**Loot boxes:**

Loot boxes — randomised in-game purchases where the content is not known before purchase — are classified as gambling where real money is spent and regulated accordingly. Purchase of loot boxes by under-18s: prohibited. Advertising of loot box mechanics to under-18s: prohibited.

# PART SEVEN: RIGHT TO DISCONNECT

**In the workplace:**

Employers cannot routinely require employees to be available, respond to messages, or perform work-related tasks outside their contracted hours. This is already in the main platform employment section — repeated here as a digital rights principle.

Outside contracted hours:
- Emails, messages, and notifications sent by employers outside contracted hours create no obligation to respond until the next contracted period
- Employees may not be penalised for not responding outside contracted hours
- On-call arrangements must be specifically contracted and compensated — incidental availability is not on-call

**In personal life:**

Platforms may not send push notifications between the hours of 10pm and 7am without explicit user consent to receive notifications at those hours. Default notification settings must be off or set to daytime only.

# WHAT COMES NEXT

Digital technology changes faster than legislation. The specific rules above address what is currently known to be harmful.

Britain Forward acknowledges that new harmful practices will emerge — and commits to:

- Annual review of the Digital Rights Framework by the Information Commissioner's Office, with powers to add new prohibited practices by secondary legislation rather than requiring a new Act for each
- A standing **Digital Consumer Advisory Panel** — including consumer representatives, disability advocates, child safety experts, and technology experts — that reviews emerging practices and recommends regulatory responses
- The Office for Evidence-Based Policy commissions ongoing research into the effects of digital platforms on wellbeing, democratic participation, and consumer choice

**The principle that does not change regardless of what technology does:**

An interface design that causes users to do things they would not have done if given a genuine, clear, honest choice is manipulation — not commerce, not innovation, not a feature. It is prohibited.

# ENFORCEMENT

**The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO)** gains new powers and substantially increased resourcing:

- Power to fine companies up to 4% of global annual turnover for systematic violations (matching GDPR-level penalties, which currently exist in law but are inconsistently enforced)
- Named senior accountability: a designated individual at each platform is legally responsible for compliance; that person is liable for systematic failures
- Power to require platforms to cease specific practices within 24 hours of a substantiated serious violation
- Public register of enforcement actions — named companies, specific violations, penalties imposed
- ICO budget substantially increased — current under-resourcing is why existing law is not enforced

**Private right of action:**

Individuals who have suffered harm from prohibited practices — financial loss, privacy violation, or significant distress — have a right to bring claims in court or through a new Digital Consumer Tribunal that does not require legal representation to access.
